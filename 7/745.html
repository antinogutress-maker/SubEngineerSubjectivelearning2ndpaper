1. Size of Aggregate

Larger aggregates have less total surface area. Therefore, they require less water for lubrication with same water content than smaller aggregates (increasing Workability) and (improving Strength up to Certain limit of Size). , too large sizes can reduce interlocking and lower strength.

2. Shape of Aggregate

Rounded aggregates offer minimum friction, yielding high workability but poor interlocking (lower strength). Angular aggregates have high friction (low workability) but provide excellent interlocking (high strength).

3. Water Content (w/c ratio)

Adding more water severely increases fluidity and increases Workability. However, excess water evaporates, leaving behind voids and capillary pores, which drastically reduces Compressive Strength (Abram's Law).

4. Grading of Aggregate

Well-graded aggregates pack closely together, leaving minimum voids. This requires less cement paste for lubrication (good workability) and creates a highly dense matrix (maximum strength).

5. Surface Texture

Smooth textures allow particles to slide easily (high workability) but create a weak bond with cement paste. Rough textures restrict movement but form an intense mechanical bond (higher strength).

6. Cement Content / Paste

A higher aggregate-to-cement ratio makes the mix lean and harsh (low workability). More cement paste provides better lubrication between aggregates and creates a stronger, denser binding matrix (higher strength).

7. Admixtures

Plasticizers / Superplasticizers disperse cement particles, vastly increasing workability without adding water, thereby maintaining or increasing strength. Accelerators and retarders chemically alter setting time and early strength development.

A Only

Affects Workability Only

Time of Transit

As time passes between mixing and placing, concrete loses moisture via evaporation and initial hydration. This causes "slump loss" and reduces workability.

Temperature & Wind

High ambient temperatures and strong winds accelerate evaporation of mixing water and speed up the chemical setting process, making the mix harsh and difficult to work with.

B Only

Affects Strength Only

Curing of Concrete

Curing prevents moisture loss, allowing the chemical process of hydration to complete fully. Proper curing is the most critical post-placement factor for achieving maximum design strength.

Age of Concrete & Loading

Hydration continues over time, meaning strength increases with age (rapidly up to 28 days, then slowly). Additionally, rapid rates of loading show slightly higher apparent strength than slow, sustained loading (which causes creep).
